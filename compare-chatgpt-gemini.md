# soft-yes-or-no vs ChatGPT or Gemini

`soft-yes-or-no` is not trying to replace a general-purpose model.

It is trying to win a narrower moment:

> "This reply sounds positive. Do I actually have movement, or am I about to chase a warm maybe?"

That is where a deterministic checker beats a blank prompt box.

## When soft-yes-or-no is the better first step

- You already have the reply and want a stable read.
- You need a fast `lock it`, `ask one clarifier`, `wait`, or `treat it as a no` call.
- You are checking dating, recruiter, sales, client, support, family, or workplace replies where tone is warm but logistics are thin.
- You want the same reply to score the same way every time.

## When ChatGPT or Gemini is still useful

- You want rewrite options or brainstorming.
- You need roleplay, coaching prompts, or longer drafting help.
- You want a broad assistant, not a narrow movement-vs-stall check.

## The practical difference

| question | soft-yes-or-no | ChatGPT / Gemini |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "Does this reply actually move?" | deterministic score + verdict | depends on prompt and model output variance |
| "Can I re-check the same reply later?" | yes, same input = same output | not reliably |
| "Do I need an account or API key?" | no | usually yes |
| "Can it rewrite my follow-up in different styles?" | no, narrow checker by design | yes |
| "Can it read the deeper recurring pattern behind the thread?" | no, move into tells for that | not from one prompt alone |

## What each tool is actually good at

- `soft-yes-or-no` is best when you need one repeatable call on whether a positive-sounding reply is real movement or polite stall.
- ChatGPT or Gemini are better when you want brainstormed follow-up drafts, roleplay, or several rewrite options after you already know what the situation is.
- `tells` is the upgrade when the real question is the recurring pattern with this person, not one isolated warm-maybe reply.
